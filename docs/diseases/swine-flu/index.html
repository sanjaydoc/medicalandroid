Swine Flu (H1N1 Influenza)

Written & medically reviewed by Dr. Sanjay Anbu, MBBS · Last reviewed 26 September 2026

Swine flu is an infection with the H1N1 strain of the influenza virus. For most people it behaves like ordinary flu, but it can be more serious in young children, pregnant women, the elderly and those with other illnesses.

Causes & risk factors

  • Infection with the H1N1 influenza A virus
  • Spread through coughs, sneezes and contaminated surfaces
  • Close contact in crowded places
  • Touching the face after touching contaminated surfaces
  • Seasonal spread, often in monsoon and winter in India

Common symptoms

  • Sudden fever and chills
  • Cough and sore throat
  • Body aches and headache
  • Tiredness and weakness
  • Runny or blocked nose
  • Sometimes nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea

Tests & diagnosis

  • Throat/nasal swab (RT-PCR) to confirm H1N1
  • Rapid influenza antigen test
  • Complete blood count (CBC)
  • Chest X-ray if breathing is affected

Treatment & management

  • Rest, plenty of fluids and fever control
  • Antiviral medicine (e.g. oseltamivir) for high-risk people or severe illness, started early
  • Isolating to avoid spreading it to others
  • Yearly flu vaccination for prevention
  • Hospital care if breathing becomes difficult

When to see a doctor urgently

  • Difficulty breathing or chest pain
  • Bluish lips, confusion or persistent high fever
  • Symptoms in a pregnant woman, infant or elderly person
  • Not improving after a few days or suddenly getting worse

Frequently asked questions

How is swine flu different from ordinary flu?

Swine flu is caused by the specific H1N1 strain. The illness feels much like seasonal flu, and the same precautions and treatment apply, but certain groups are at higher risk of complications.

Is there a vaccine for swine flu?

Yes — the seasonal flu vaccine includes protection against H1N1. Yearly vaccination is the best prevention, especially for high-risk groups.

When should antivirals be used?

Antivirals work best when started early and are usually reserved for high-risk people or those with severe illness, on a doctor’s advice.
