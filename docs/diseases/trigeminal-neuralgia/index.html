Trigeminal Neuralgia

Trigeminal neuralgia causes sudden, severe, electric-shock-like facial pain on one side, often triggered by light touch, chewing or a breeze. It comes from irritation of the trigeminal nerve.

Causes & risk factors

  • A blood vessel pressing on the trigeminal nerve
  • Nerve irritation or damage
  • Occasionally linked to multiple sclerosis
  • Rarely, a growth pressing on the nerve

Common symptoms

  • Sudden stabbing or electric-shock facial pain
  • Pain on one side of the face
  • Attacks triggered by touch, chewing or wind
  • Short bursts lasting seconds to minutes
  • Pain-free periods between attacks

Tests & diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis (history and exam)
  • MRI brain

Treatment & management

  • Specific nerve-pain medicines (ordinary painkillers rarely help)
  • Adjusting medicine doses under specialist guidance
  • Procedures on the nerve if medicine does not control pain
  • Surgery to move the blood vessel off the nerve in selected cases
  • Identifying and avoiding personal triggers

When to see a doctor urgently

  • Severe facial pain interfering with eating, drinking or daily life
  • Facial pain with weakness, numbness, vision changes or a rash — get urgent review
  • Pain that is not controlled by current treatment
  • A first episode of severe facial pain that needs a diagnosis

Frequently asked questions

Why do ordinary painkillers not work?

Trigeminal neuralgia is nerve pain, which responds to specific nerve-calming medicines rather than standard painkillers. A doctor can prescribe and adjust the right type.

Can it be cured?

Many people are well controlled with medicine, and surgery can give long-lasting relief in suitable cases, especially when a blood vessel is pressing on the nerve.
