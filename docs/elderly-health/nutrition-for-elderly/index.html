Nutrition for the elderly

Written & medically reviewed by Dr. Sanjay Anbu, MBBS · Last reviewed 26 September 2026

Eating well protects strength, immunity and independence in older age. Appetite often falls, so the focus shifts to nutrient- and protein-rich foods, enough fluids, and preventing unintended weight loss.

What matters

  • Enough protein (dal, curd, paneer, eggs, milk) to preserve muscle
  • Small, frequent, nutrient-dense meals if appetite is low
  • Enough fluids — thirst is blunted with age (dehydration risk)
  • Calcium, vitamin D, B12 and fibre
  • Soft, easy-to-chew foods if teeth or swallowing are a problem

When to see a doctor urgently

  • Unintended weight loss (find the cause — see a doctor)
  • Difficulty or pain swallowing, or coughing when eating/drinking
  • Signs of dehydration (confusion, little urine)
  • Poor appetite with low mood (possible depression)
Protein and fluids matter most. Protecting muscle with enough protein and staying hydrated help older adults stay strong and independent. Unintended weight loss should always be investigated. MedDroid gives general information, not a diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

What should elderly people eat?

Protein-rich foods (dal, curd, paneer, eggs, milk), plenty of fluids, and calcium, vitamin D, B12 and fibre — in small, frequent, nutrient-dense meals if appetite is low.

How can I help an elderly person with poor appetite?

Offer small frequent meals, nutrient-dense foods and favourites, make mealtimes social, treat mouth/teeth problems, and check for depression or illness behind the loss of appetite.

Why is protein important for the elderly?

It preserves muscle strength, which protects against frailty and falls. Older adults often need more protein than they get.
